# Fire spread between items and between rooms: evidence with numbers

Prepared for the NIBS Duplex whole-house fire engine (20 cm grid, items ignite on critical flux held long enough, then burn along measured curves).

**How to read the tags.** Every number carries its source. The read status is marked as:
- **[read]**: I read the original PDF text, table or figure myself. Page numbers are the printed page numbers unless "PDF p." is given.
- **[abstract]**: I saw only an abstract or a landing page.
- **[secondary]**: The number comes from a search snippet or another author's summary, and I did not verify it.
- **(my arithmetic)**: A number I derived from the sources. It is not a measurement.

## 1. Item-to-item spread

### 1.1 Babrauskas 1981, *Will the Second Item Ignite?* (NBSIR 81-2271) [read, full report]

**Method** (pp. 2–10):
- The first item was burned on a load cell. Gardon gauges behind 150° Irtran-2 windows measured only radiation, not convection. They sat at heights of 0.41, 0.86, 1.32 and 1.78 m, and at 0.05, 0.20, 0.50, 1.12 and 1.73 m from the item's leading edge.
- Second-item materials were tested for piloted ignition (spark) at uniform 20 and 40 kW/m² in a modified ISO ignitability apparatus.
- The ignition source was a polyethylene wastebasket holding 12 milk cartons: about 1.8 g/s ≈ 52.5 kW for about 200 s. At 0.05 m from it, at 0.41 m height, the irradiance was only 8 kW/m². Items ignited only when the basket was flush against them (p. 7).
- Fang's data, quoted on p. 5, suggest that close to the source a convective component of 10–20% of the radiant flux should be expected. In the immediate vicinity, convection can dominate.

**Target ignitability categories** (p. 9, and Fig. 13 on p. 27):
- 20 kW/m² is "near the minimum ignition flux for many common combustibles".
- Items needing 40 kW/m² are "difficult-to-ignite".
- For "especially easily ignitable combinations", even 10 kW/m² may suffice (citing Moulen & Grubits 1973).
- Fig. 13 labels the three curves: "Especially easily ignitable (10 kW/m²)", "Normal ignition range (20 kW/m²)" and "Difficult to ignite (40 kW/m²)".

**Table 2: irradiance mapping** (p. 13; I rotated and read the scanned table). The distance columns are the maximum distance from the item's edge at which each flux was reached, interpolated between gauge positions.

Peak irradiance usually occurred at 0.41 m height, and at 0.86 m for some items. The upholstered items had polyurethane foam unless noted otherwise.

**Table 3: piloted ignition times** (p. 22). Most fabric/PU-foam items ignited in 18–43 s at 20 kW/m² and in 9–24 s at 40 kW/m². The case goods did not ignite at 20 kW/m² and took longer at 40 kW/m²: desk F09 110 s, cabinets F10 236 s and F11 191 s, fiberglass chair F14 455 s. Prison chair F18 did not ignite even at 40 kW/m².
- p. 21 rule of thumb: "if more than about 40 s is required for ignition at 40 kW/m², then ignition at 20 kW/m² is unlikely."
- Ignition time scaled closer to 1/q than to the 1/q² expected for thermally thick materials.
- Footnote on p. 21: wool over neoprene foam needs about 65 kW/m².

**Conclusions** (pp. 24–29):
- The fastest-burning items gave 40–80 kW/m² at 0.05 m, with peak ṁ of 20–47 g/s. Babrauskas assumed 21 kJ/g, which he states as "very rough", giving about 0.4–1.0 MW (p. 24).
- **40 kW/m² was not recorded farther than 0.44 m away, and 20 kW/m² was not found beyond 0.88 m.** Common furnishings needing about 20 kW/m² "would stand little hazard … if placed at least 1 m away", but only before flashover (p. 28).
- Peak irradiances of 40 and 57 kW/m² at 0.05 m produced 20 kW/m² at up to 0.20 m and 0.50 m respectively (p. 29).
- Fabric/PU items ignite in under 45 s at 20 kW/m², which is short compared with peak burning durations of several minutes. So any first item giving more than 20 kW/m² would ignite them (p. 25).
- Laminate cabinets produced high mass-loss rates but only 6–8 kW/m² to the side. They would not ignite side neighbours, but could ignite items in front or overhead (p. 24).
- **Point source:** "the burning item does not act as a point source and … a 1/r² flux representation would not be appropriate in the near region" (p. 11, from Fig. 10, wicker couch F19).

**Fig. 13** (p. 27): ignition distance against the first item's peak ṁ. These are my read-off of the plotted points, accurate to about ±0.05 m:
- 10 kW/m² curve: ≈0.88 m at 12 g/s, ≈1.38 m at 47 g/s.
- 20 kW/m² curve: ≈0.16 m at 6 g/s, 0.29 m at 12 g/s, 0.43 m at 18 g/s, 0.88 m at 47 g/s.
- 40 kW/m² curve: ≈0 at about 6 g/s, rising nearly linearly to 0.40 m at 47 g/s.

I did not find a published closed-form equation for these curves in the report. A search for a "Q = 30 + 3000·D" style correlation found nothing I could verify, so I do not give one.

### 1.2 Babrauskas & Krasny 1985, *Fire Behavior of Upholstered Furniture* (NBS Monograph 173), §5.7.3 Multiple item burning [read, PDF pp. 52–54, printed pp. 42–44]

- **Fuel packages** (p. 43): contiguous or stacked items "burn as one discrete item". If non-contiguous items only ignite one another, the simplest model is the **sum of the isolated burning rates**.
- **Flame merging** (p. 43): flames merge "when the clear spacing is less than about 0.2 of the flame height" (from studies of simple fuel arrays, ref. 284). For sizeable furniture, flame heights are about 1–5 times the object width.
- **Radius beyond which ignition is unlikely** (p. 44): "about 1.5 m is a limit beyond which second item involvement will be unlikely prior to flashover." Larger first fires either ignite objects through "long flames which fold over and continue along the ceiling" or lead to flashover.
- Peak fluxes from upholstered furniture occur at about 0.5 m height (p. 44).
- **Far-field correlations** (p. 44, eqs. 21–22):
  - Vertical targets: q″ = 0.32 ṁ / r^1.8 for r > 1.3 m (Mizuno & Kawagoe). The monograph warns that "this should not be extrapolated to smaller r distances, since excessive q″ values would be predicted".
  - Horizontal targets: q″ = 0.46 ṁ / r² (Ahonen et al.).
  - The units of ṁ are as defined in the monograph. I believe ṁ is in g/s, but I did not confirm it in the nomenclature.
- **Room augmentation:**
  - Bed fire in a 2.4 × 3.7 × 2.4 m room: the second (mattress) peak was 1430 kW in the room against 1090–1210 kW in open burns, i.e. **15–30% augmentation after flashover** (p. 42).
  - Chair and loveseat room fires showed **no significant enhancement** over free burn, within about ±15% (p. 43).

### 1.3 Ohlemiller & Gann 2003, NIST TN 1446, *Estimating Reduced Fire Risk Resulting From an Improved Mattress Flammability Standard* [read: executive summary, pp. 11–14, 19, 25–26, 29]

- **Two spread mechanisms are named:** "Ignition of additional items by a burning bed will either be by direct flame impingement (contiguous items) or by fire-generated thermal radiation (separated items)" (exec. summary).
- **Flame contact flux:** "flame contact fluxes are of the order of 30 kW/m² or more" (p. 11).
- **Lateral flame reach from the side of a burning bed** (p. 19, open hood):
  - M3 bed (bedclothes fire only): mean instantaneous reach 20.4 cm (s.d. 2.0); mean reach lasting at least 1 s was 15.8 cm (s.d. 1.6).
  - M5 twin: 39.1 cm instantaneous (s.d. 5.2) and 31.4 cm lasting 1 s (s.d. 1.9).
  - The authors give a less than 1% probability of instantaneous reach beyond 54 cm for M5. Flames also act as a **pilot** for vapours from nearby objects.
- **Piloted radiative ignition reach** for M3 beds: about 50 cm in all three tests (p. 25).
- **Susceptible targets:** "Fabric-like objects (upholstered chairs, a second bed, draperies) are at risk of ignition down to incident heat fluxes of about 10 kW/m²; such flux levels can be experienced far from the burning bed." Wood needs higher flux (exec. summary p. 6; I checked the page image).
- **Pilot matters:** surrogate samples (newsprint, terry cloth) that the model said should be "near ignition" **did not ignite** without a pilot. They only turned medium to dark brown (p. 26). Non-piloted reach for M3/M5 "pull[s] back inside the flame reach peripheries" (p. 29).
- **Flashover thresholds:**
  - "For ordinary-size bedrooms, a peak heat release rate larger than about 1000 kW will lead to flashover."
  - A bed over 500 kW plus bedclothes of up to 400 kW gives about 900 kW. "Secondary ignition of contiguous items … could readily push the fire over the flashover limit."
  - Open-hood bed peaks ranged from 160 kW (M3 twin) to 3850 kW (M1 king) (exec. summary).
- **Closed room** (p. 14, footnote): burning less than 0.4 kg of material in a small closed 9 m² room can create an incapacitating atmosphere. A nearly or fully closed door "can put the fire out while leaving a very toxic atmosphere".

### 1.4 How often is spread by radiation, by flame contact, or by dripping?

- **I found no study that gives quantitative shares.**
- Qualitative statements I read:
  - TN 1446 (above): contiguous items are ignited by flame contact, separated items by radiation.
  - Fleury et al. 2011 (p. 1) quote Karlsson & Quintiere (2000): "The dominant mechanism for the spread of fire within buildings is direct thermal radiation from the existing flames." This is a secondary quote; I did not read Karlsson & Quintiere.
- **Dripping and melt flow** [read: TN 1493 abstract; Monograph 173 pp. 21, 27]:
  - Ohlemiller & Shields 2008 (NIST TN 1493): PU foam and thermoplastics produce a liquid phase that feeds a pool fire and "enhances the rate of heat release". The fraction of mass lost as melt flow "vary over a wide range" between foams.
  - Monograph 173: thermoplastic fabrics "melt and shrink … The molten area tends to form a burning bead". A melt bead "burned ahead of the PU fire front, causing more PU ignition". Polypropylene frames melt and collapse early.
- **Observed spread routes in UL room tests** (Kerber 2012, §3.1.2) [read]. Modern rooms:
  - The fire spread from sofa to throw, to lamp shade, then to carpet "adjacent to the sofa". Carpet ignition is plausibly from burning drips or pool, but the paper does not say so.
  - Then to the TV stand.
  - The far sofa arm was **ignited "from radiant energy from the gas layer"** at 4:16, about 30 s before flashover at 4:45.
  - The floor just inside the opening was ignited by flames leaving the top of the opening. This was the paper's flashover marker.
- **Legacy rooms:** the fire went from sofa to curtains behind the sofa by contact. In the 4.0 × 5.5 m legacy room, items not adjacent to the sofa **never ignited** because the gas layer never got hot enough.

### 1.5 Flame height and near-field radiation

- **Heskestad flame height:**
  - As used in CFAST 7 (Tech Ref eq. 3.19) [read]: L/D = −1.02 + 3.7 Q̇*^(2/5).
  - As quoted by Fleury et al. 2011, eq. 3 [read]: H = 0.23 Q^(2/5) − 1.02 D, with Q in kW and D, H in m. The usual textbook coefficient is 0.235; Fleury prints 0.23.
  - **Example (my arithmetic):** Q = 1 MW, D = 1 m gives L ≈ 0.235·15.85 − 1.02 ≈ 2.7 m. That exceeds a 2.4 m ceiling, so flames fold along the ceiling, which is Monograph 173's "long flames" route.
- **Where the point source sits in different models:**
  - CFAST 7 puts the point source at **1/3 of the Heskestad flame height** (Tech Ref §5.1.5) [read]. Its stated limitation: "Radiation from fires is modeled with a simple point source approximation. This limits the accuracy of the model within a few diameters of the fire" (Tech Ref, limitations list).
  - Fleury et al. 2011 put it at **mid flame height** (H/2) [read].
- **Fleury, Spearpoint & Fleischmann 2011, FEMTC** [read]: six radiation models were compared with Schmidt-Boelter measurements around a 100–300 kW propane burner (300 mm unit length, aspect ratios 1:1 to 3:1) at 0.5, 0.75, 1.0, 1.5 and 2.0 m.
  - The **point source was the most accurate and most robust** model: mean error 29% over all data, 18% excluding horizontal targets.
  - Point source was best below 10 kW/m². Above 10 kW/m², Shokri & Beyler was marginally better.
  - The common advice to restrict the point source to fluxes of 5 kW/m² or less (Beyler 2002) "was found to be irrelevant in this application".
  - B-RISK adopted the point source for this reason.
- **Measured near-field caps:**
  - Babrauskas 1981: maximum about 80 kW/m² at 0.05 m from the item (Table 2).
  - Monograph 173: Mizuno & Kawagoe's 1/r^1.8 law overpredicts if extrapolated below 1.3 m.
  - Together these say a pure 1/R² law with R measured to the item centre misbehaves within about 0.5–1 m of large items. The error can go either way, depending on where the source point sits.

## 2. Room scale: smoke layer, flashover, ventilation

### 2.1 Kerber 2012, *Analysis of Changing Residential Fire Dynamics and Its Implications on Firefighter Operational Timeframes*, Fire Technology 48:865–891 [read, full paper]

- **Rooms:**
  - Experiments 1–4: 3.7 × 3.7 × 2.4 m, with a 2.4 × 2.1 m front opening.
  - Experiments 5–6: 4.0 × 5.5 × 2.4 m, with a 3.0 × 2.1 m opening.
  - Fuel loads (Table 2): Exp. 3 19.0 kg/m², Exp. 4 22.9 kg/m², Exp. 5–6 11.2 kg/m².
  - All fires were started with a candle on the sofa.
- **Table 8, flashover times** (modern vs legacy):

| Pair | Modern | Legacy |
|---|---|---|
| 1, 2 | 3:40 | 29:30 |
| 3, 4 | 4:45 | 34:15 |
| 5, 6 | 3:20 | not achieved |

  The paper's summary: "living room fires have flashover times of less than 5 min when they used to be on the order of 30 min" (abstract). Legacy rooms took "at least 700% longer".
- **HRR (Fig. 18):**
  - Exp. 3 (modern) peaked at about **7.5 MW at 450 s**. Exp. 4 (legacy) peaked at about **6 MW at 2200 s**.
  - Total energy is printed as "3.2 MJ" and "3.5 MJ". This is almost certainly GJ: UL 2013 measured 3580–4330 MJ for similar rooms.
- **Wall linings** (§3.2.1): in a floor-furnace test, 12.7 mm unrated gypsum under 2×10 joists was **breached at 23:30**; plaster and lath was breached at about 74 min. The deflection criterion L/240 was reached at 35:30 vs 75:45.
  - In room tests "gypsum wallboard fails at the seams". Joint compound dries and falls out, and heat then ignites the back paper and the studs.
  - "This change in lining material allows for easier transition from content fire to structure fire as the fire has a path into void spaces."
- **Floors** (Table 3, standard time-temperature curve):
  - Unprotected 2×10 joists: collapse/breach at 18:35. Unprotected engineered I-joists: 6:00.
  - With 12.7 mm gypsum ceiling: 2×10 at 44:40; I-joist at 26:43; metal gusset truss at 29; finger-joint truss at 26:30.
- **Windows** (Table 5, UL 9 furnace curve; failure = at least 25% of glass area open):
  - Legacy single-glazed: average 9:37 (577 s) and 14:06 (846 s).
  - Modern double-glazed: average 4:14 to 5:12 (254–312 s).
- **Interior doors** (Table 6, UL 10C furnace curve; failure = sustained burning on the unexposed face; **one test only**):
  - Hollow oak 5:12 (312 s), hollow composite 5:15 (315 s), solid 6-panel 5:02 (302 s).
  - The solid door burned through only at its thinner panels.
  - "The fire containment ability of interior doors during a well-ventilated compartment fire is approximately 5 min."
  - Conclusion: "If a fire in a closed room is able to get air to burn from a failed window, then it can burn through a door and extend to the rest of the house."

### 2.2 Kerber 2013, UL FSRI, *Study of the Effectiveness of Fire Service Vertical Ventilation and Suppression Tactics in Single Family Homes* [read: exec. summary, §4, §5.8 excerpts, §6.1, §6.2, §6.6, §6.9]

The houses were a one-storey ranch (1200 ft², 3 bedrooms, 8 rooms) and a two-storey house (3200 ft², 4 bedrooms, open plan, two-storey great room), built inside UL's Northbrook fire facility. The report has 529 pages.

- **Room calorimeter tests** (§4.5, Table 4.1): 18 × 13 ft rooms with a 10 × 7 ft opening.

| Room | Time to flashover (m:ss) | Peak HRR (MW) | Total heat released (MJ) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Living room | 4:45 | 8.8 | 4060 |
| 2008 living room, 12 ft opening | 5:00 | 11.5 | 3650 |
| Bedroom | 5:30 (flaming started at 2:10) | 9.4 | 3580 |
| 16 ft tall living room | 5:20 | 9.8 | 4330 |

  - The authors take the peak HRR to be opening-limited at about **9.3 ± 0.5 MW**.
  - Doubling the room volume (16 ft ceiling) delayed flashover by only about 35 s.
- **Modern vs legacy, after ventilating a ventilation-limited fire** (§6.1):
  - Peak temperatures before the fire became ventilation-limited were 1100 °F (modern) vs 450 °F (legacy).
  - Minimum O₂ before ventilation was 5% vs 18%.
  - **Time from ventilation to flashover was about 2 min (modern) vs over 8 min (legacy).**
  - §5.8 (p. ~229): about 2 min after a window opened in Exp. 1 and 9. In Exp. 17 (legacy), about 9 min vs under 3 min in Exp. 1.
  - Several one-storey experiments went from tenable (500 °F at 3 ft) to flashover in **less than 20 s** (Table 5.58 discussion).
- **Door control** (§6.2): limiting the inlet "limits the heat able to be released … flashover was delayed for minutes". The doorway "is a choke point": hot gas leaves through the top and air enters low (§6.6).
- **Spread along flow paths** (§6.6):
  - Once the fire room flashes over, "flames and hot gases will exit the room and spread toward the vents. If other fuels are in this path, they will ignite and increase the HRR rapidly because they are in a preheated environment with additional unburned fuel".
  - The original room then dies down because the new room takes its oxygen.
  - Kitchen and dining room "are elevated in temperature, but are not burning … due to the lack of oxygen", until their windows are opened or fail.
- **A counter-example** (two-storey, Bedroom 3 fire, p. ~215): after roof ventilation the bedroom flashed over in about 4 min and "flames flowed out of Bedroom 3 and over the hallway to the roof vent. **Ignition did not take place outside of the bedroom**."
- **Line of sight** (§5.8.13.2): the den "is the only room with no visual access to the family room … since there is no radiation from the fire room to the den, [it] consistently results in lower temperatures".

### 2.3 Ventilation limit and flashover correlations (Babrauskas & Krasny 1985, Monograph 173) [read, printed pp. 42, 44]

- **Stoichiometric (Kawagoe-type) limit:** q̇_st ≈ 1500 A_v√h_v (kW; eq. 18), from about 13.1 MJ/kg O₂ (eq. 17).
  - Fuel pyrolysed beyond q̇_st/ΔH_c "cannot be burned within the room, but is available for burning outside the room."
  - The excess pyrolysate is about 30% for wood cribs; for upholstered furniture it is "an open question".
- **Flashover:**
  - Rough estimate: q̇ ≥ 750 A_v√h_v (eq. 23). This is consistent with "about 50 percent of q̇_st".
  - Better (Thomas-type): q̇ ≥ 378 A_v√h_v + 7.8 A_w, where A_w is the wall plus ceiling area (eq. 20).
  - Babrauskas 1981 (p. 24): a common doorway has A√h ≈ 2.2 m^5/2, so a 0.4–1.0 MW item alone is unlikely to flash a room ventilated by a door. A window with A√h ≈ 1.0 m^5/2 makes flashover possible.
  - Monograph 173 worked example (p. 44): a 2.5 × 3.7 × 2.5 m room with a 1.25 m², 0.8 m-high window needs q̇ ≈ 740 kW for flashover.
- **Example for a 0.9 × 2.0 m door (my arithmetic):** A√H ≈ 1.8 × 1.414 ≈ 2.55 m^5/2. That gives about 3.8 MW stoichiometric and about 1.9 MW by the 750 rule.

### 2.4 Smoke layer radiation

- **B-RISK** (Wade et al. 2016, BRANZ SR364 §4.3.2) [read]:
  - Upper-layer flux to the **top surface** of an object: q″_U = ε_U σ T_U⁴ F.
  - F is the view factor of a rectangle (room L × W) to a parallel element at distance z(t) below the layer interface. It is taken at the room centre, which is conservative, and set to 1 once the layer reaches the top of the object.
  - ε_U = 1 − e^(−kL) + ε_g e^(−kL), with k = soot concentration × specific extinction, and mean beam length L = 3.6 V_U/A_U.
  - "The first case [flame radiation] is likely to dominate where secondary objects are relatively close … The second case [layer radiation] is likely to dominate for more remote items."
- **BRANZFIRE** (Wade 2004, SR92 §4.11) [read]: flashover is defined as floor radiation above 20 kW/m², which switches the model to the post-flashover (COMPF2) model.
- **Rough check (my arithmetic):** with F = 1 and ε = 1, a layer gives 20 kW/m² at about 770 K (≈500 °C). With realistic ε below 1 and F below 1, the 600 °C layer rule and the 20 kW/m² floor rule are consistent.
- **Observed:** gas-layer radiation ignited the far sofa arm about 30 s before flashover (Kerber 2012, Exp. 3).

## 3. Room-to-room spread

### 3.1 Open vs closed doors

- **UL FSRI, *Close Before You Doze* full-scale test (Weinschenk, 27 Nov 2017)** [read, FSRI web page Table 1]. Peak values before suppression, 4 ft above the floor, in a 1200 ft² ranch:

| Location | Temperature (°F) | CO (ppm) |
|---|---|---|
| Living room | 1400 | N/A |
| Hallway | 750 | 10,000 |
| Open bedroom | 500 | 6,000 |
| Closed bedroom | 100 | 600 |

- **Kerber 2013, §6.9, Tables 6.1–6.2** [read]: every experiment had one closed bedroom next to an open bedroom, with untenability judged at 3 ft above the floor.
  - Open bedrooms, CO untenable:
    - One-storey: 5:51–7:04 in 6 of 9 experiments. The exceptions were 11:54 in Exp. 13, 29:04 in Exp. 17 (legacy contents), and one equipment malfunction.
    - Two-storey: 10:50–13:22, and 18:54 in Exp. 16.
  - Open bedrooms, temperature untenable:
    - One-storey: 4:58–7:29, except 16:16 in Exp. 9 and 29:13 in Exp. 17.
    - Two-storey: 7:31–9:04, except 27:05 in Exp. 16.
  - Closed bedrooms were tenable throughout, except CO at 19:33 (one-storey Exp. 15) and 32:14 (two-storey Exp. 16).
- **Kerber 2013, Table 5.27** (one-storey Exp. 5, temperatures at 3 ft, °F):
  - Closed Bedroom 3: 77, then 78, then 80 (+4%).
  - At the same times the living room went 260, then 400, then 1653, and the hallway 222, then 225, then 590.
- **Door burn-through:** about 5 min for hollow-core and 6-panel solid doors under the UL 10C furnace curve (Kerber 2012, Table 6; one test). That is a well-ventilated furnace exposure. Behind a door in a ventilation-limited house, the exposure can be lower. The tenable closed bedrooms above suggest doors held through these experiments of up to about 30 min. That is my inference: the report does not state door integrity.

### 3.2 Hot gas, flames into corridor, ceiling jets

- **Flow-path behaviour** (UL 2013 §6.6, quoted in §2.2 above): flames follow the path to the vent, and fuels on that path ignite. Rooms off the path heat up but are air-starved. Ignition outside the fire room is not guaranteed (Bedroom 3 case).
- **Flame out of a doorway:** Kerber 2012 Exp. 1 used ignition of flooring just inside the opening, by flames from the top of the opening, as the flashover marker. UL 2013 measured flows out of the roof vent of about 20–25 mph, rising to 30–40 mph after flashover (p. ~205).
- **Ceiling jet** (Alpert/Heskestad, as implemented in CFAST 7 Tech Ref §3.5, eqs. 3.29–3.32) [read]:
  - ΔT_cj = T∞·Q̇*_H^(2/3)·6.3 for r/H ≤ 0.2, and T∞·Q̇*_H^(2/3)·(0.225 + 0.27 r/H)^(−4/3) for 0.2 < r/H < 4.
  - v_cj = √(gH)·Q̇*_H^(1/3)·3.61 for r/H ≤ 0.17, and 1.06 (r/H)^(−0.69) for 0.17 < r/H < 4.
  - Q̇*_H = Q̇ / (ρ∞ c_p T∞ √g H^(5/2)). T∞ is replaced by the upper-layer temperature in a room with a layer.
  - CFAST also has a corridor-flow treatment (§3.5, text mentions corridors); I did not extract it.

### 3.3 Through walls, floors, voids

- Gypsum fails at the seams and opens a path into stud cavities. The 12.7 mm unrated board was breached at 23:30 in a floor-furnace test (Kerber 2012 §3.2.1).
- **Component Additive Method** (AWC FAQ page) [read]: one layer of 5/8″ Type X gypsum is assigned 40 min, and 2×4 wood studs 20 min.
  - 1/2″ regular gypsum = 15 min appeared only in search snippets of AWC DCA 4 [secondary, unverified].
- I found **no quantitative residential test of fire spreading room-to-room through walls or voids** within the first 30 min.

## 4. Rules used in zone and field models

- **CFAST 7** (NIST TN 1889v1 Tech Ref, TN 1889v2 User's Guide; GitHub release 7.7.7) [read]:
  - Fires are user-specified HRR "objects" with no plume interaction between fires. CFAST does **not** predict fire growth or spread.
  - A secondary object ignites by **time, target surface temperature, or target incident heat flux**: "Target is typically placed at the base of the fire to be ignited" (UG §6.1).
  - Vents (doors, windows) can open or close on time, target temperature, or target flux. This is how window breakage and door failure are represented.
  - Radiation from the fire uses a point source at 1/3 Heskestad flame height, plus radiation from walls and layers. **Radiative exchange between compartments is not modelled.**
  - Oxygen limit: Q̇ = min(ṁ_f ΔH, ṁ_e Y_O2 C_LOL ΔH_O2), with a tanh smoothing about Y_O2,l = 0.15 by mass.
  - Unburned fuel is carried in the upper layer. It burns when it reaches oxygen, including after spilling into another compartment (door-jet-like behaviour; Tech Ref eqs. 3.14–3.16).
- **FDS 6** (NIST SP 1019, User's Guide §9.2.3, release 6.11.1) [read]: "Thermally-thick solids that burn at a specified rate".
  - A SURF with HRRPUA + IGNITION_TEMPERATURE + RAMP_Q ignites each wall cell when its **surface temperature** reaches the ignition temperature (example: 500 °C). It then follows a prescribed HRR ramp measured from that cell's ignition time.
  - Optional parameters: EXTINCTION_TEMPERATURE and HEAT_OF_VAPORIZATION.
  - SPREAD_RATE (m/s) can force radial spread over a surface.
  - Item-to-item and room-to-room spread then emerge from the computed heat fluxes.
- **B-RISK / BRANZFIRE design fire generator** (Wade et al. 2016, SR364 §4.3.2; model by Baker et al. 2011) [read]. This is the closest match to our engine.
  - Items are rectilinear prisms.
  - Flame flux uses a point source: q″ = Q̇ λ_r / (4π r²). r is the **plan distance from the burning item's centre to the nearest point of the target**, found by sampling 10 points on each side. Flux from all burning items is summed.
  - The upper-layer flux to the target's top surface is computed concurrently (§2.4 above).
  - Ignition uses the **Flux-Time Product**: FTP = Σ (q″_i − q″_cr)^n Δt_i, accumulated only while q″_i > q″_cr. The item ignites when FTP exceeds its value.
  - Each item has separate (FTP, q″_cr, n) sets, with n between 1 and 2, for "vertical surface by source items" and "horizontal surface by upper layer". These come from cone tests, piloted or auto-ignition (Silcock & Shields 1995, via SR92 §5.2).
  - Total HRR is the **sum of free-burn item curves** (as in FPETool FREEBURN). An optional burning-rate enhancement sub-model exists (§6.8).
- **BRANZFIRE 2004** (SR92) [read]:
  - FTP ignition for wall linings.
  - Flashover at 20 kW/m² on the floor.
  - Oxygen limits of 10% near room temperature and 2% near flashover temperatures (§4.10).

## 5. What this means for our engine

1. **Use three ignition zones, not one.**
   - **Flame contact:** the target is within the burning item's lateral flame reach or flame volume. That is about 0.2–0.4 m (1–2 cells) beside a burning bed (TN 1446, p. 19), more under ceiling fold-over. Treat it as at least 30 kW/m² (TN 1446) plus a pilot, so ignition takes seconds.
   - **Direct radiation** from flames.
   - **Upper-layer radiation** to top surfaces (B-RISK).
   - The point source is acceptable: it was the best of six models in Fleury 2011. But place the source at 1/3 to 1/2 of the Heskestad flame height, measure R to the target's nearest face, and cap the near-field flux at about 80 kW/m², the highest Babrauskas measured at 0.05 m.
2. **Replace "flux > critical for long enough" with a Flux-Time Product accumulator:** FTP += (q″ − q″_cr)^n · Δt, and ignite when FTP ≥ FTP_item. This is how B-RISK does it. It handles fluctuating flux, and q″_cr from NUREG-1805 can be reused directly.
3. **Validate against Babrauskas 1981.**
   - Run single items at the Table 2 peak ṁ (×21 kJ/g, Babrauskas's own rough conversion) and check that 20 kW/m² ends at 0.1–0.9 m and that nothing beyond about 1 m (Babrauskas) or 1.5 m (Monograph 173) ignites before flashover.
   - Fabric/PU targets should ignite in about 20–45 s at 20 kW/m².
   - At the 20 cm grid, those thresholds are only 1–4 cells, so rasterising distances to cell centres can shift outcomes by one cell.
4. **Piloted vs unpiloted ignition matters.** TN 1446 targets that the model predicted would ignite did not ignite without a pilot. Treat critical-flux ignition as piloted only when a flame or ember is nearby (flame-contact zone, falling burning material). Otherwise require a higher unpiloted threshold, for which I did not collect numbers.
5. **Add a ventilation cap per room.**
   - Q_room ≤ 1500 A√H kW, summed over open openings.
   - Excess pyrolysate leaves as unburned fuel and burns where it meets air: at the doorway, in the corridor, or at the next vent. This is CFAST's approach and the UL flow-path observations.
   - When a door or window opens on a ventilation-limited room, expect flashover about 2 min later with modern contents (UL 2013).
6. **Flashover check per room:** upper layer 600 °C or 20 kW/m² at the floor (what you already have), cross-checked with Thomas, 378 A√H + 7.8 A_w. Expect about 3–5 min from a sofa ignition in a modern living room, and about 30 min or never with legacy contents (Kerber 2012).
7. **Room-to-room spread happens through gas and flame along the flow path, not through walls, in the first 20–25 min.**
   - Hot layer and flames spread through open doors toward vents and ignite items in their path, preferentially items with line of sight to the fire (UL den observation).
   - Air-starved neighbouring rooms stay hot but unburned until a window fails, at about 4–5 min for modern double glazing and 10–14 min for legacy glazing under furnace exposure.
   - Ignition outside the fire room is **not** automatic (UL Bedroom 3 case).
8. **Closed doors are close to perfect barriers on hackathon time scales.**
   - Behind a closed door, use about 100 °F and 600 ppm CO, against 500 °F and 6000 ppm with the door open (CBYD).
   - A door can fail by burn-through at about 5 min under full furnace exposure (Kerber 2012, single test). Model it as a vent that opens on a target-flux or temperature trigger, the way CFAST does.
   - Gypsum breach of about 23 min means through-wall spread can be ignored for short runs.
9. **Don't overclaim on drips and mechanism shares.** No source quantifies how often radiation, contact or drips is the spread mechanism. If dripping is modelled, it should be a qualitative "burning melt spreads to floor/carpet" rule, labelled as not calibrated.
